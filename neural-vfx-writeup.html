Neural VFX · Field notes

VFX on real footage:
what works, what drifts, and why.

A build log about transforming real footage with generative AI — the parts that worked, the defects that only show up on playback, and the one hard problem underneath all of it: generative video has no sense of space.

The premise

Real footage, not generated video.

There are two very different games people call "AI video." One is generating a clip from a prompt — the model invents everything: subject, motion, world. The other is VFX on real footage — you start with a real shot (a real person, real motion, real camera, real light) and transform part of it while keeping the rest. The source atoms are real. That constraint is the whole point, and it changes every problem.

Our test case: take a real performance and recast the performer as a character — keep the exact motion, timing, and scene — then optionally drop them into a new world. Simple to say. The interesting part is everything that goes wrong, because each failure teaches you something about the gap between generative models and traditional 3D.

Part 1

The recast, and the tyranny of the still frame.

The recast engine takes a driver video (the real performance) and a character reference image, and paints the character into the driver's silhouette, frame by frame. One rule dominates everything:

The reference drives the result. The prompt is a whisper.

On a distilled, low-guidance path the reference controls identity, colors, and look; the text prompt barely moves the needle — except in a few sharp, surprising ways. Three bit us:

  • The reference's aspect ratio leaks. The transfer composites the reference at its native scale, center-anchored, unresized. Feed a wide reference into a portrait render and its center crop floats into frame as a giant ghost. Fix: match the reference to the output aspect.
  • A named color in the prompt overrides the reference. A stray "white cap" phrase beat the reference image and turned a colored hat white — every frame. The prompt is a whisper, but say a color out loud and it shouts.
  • Stocky characters stretch on extreme poses. The model fills the driver's silhouette, so a short, stocky character gets stretched tall and thin to match a full-extension move. A pose-strength parameter is the lever — lower it and the character keeps its own build.

Then the defect that taught the real lesson. The source clip had a small watermark that moved — our static mask missed it, and the model baked it onto the character's shoe. We'd been checking quality on still frames, and the stills passed. The watermark, a prop that reappeared after being tossed, a limb clipping off-frame, slow color ramps — every defect that shipped was invisible in a frame check and obvious on playback.

Rule zero of AI-video QA: the played video is the gate, never sampled stills.

Which raises a problem. You can't watch every frame of every iteration. So we built something to watch for us.

Part 2

A multi-model auditor, pointed at video.

There's a well-worn trick in LLM work: run one model's output through a second, independent model as an auditor. It catches what the first one — and you — missed. We applied it to video, as a two-layer gate every render has to pass.

Layer 1 — deterministic, local, free. A small script flags the mechanical, temporal defects stills hide: saturation ramps and grade jumps, hard seams (frame-to-frame diff spikes, correlated to render-chunk boundaries), and edge-clipping. A cheap tripwire that exits non-zero to block a bad deliverable in CI.

Layer 2 — an independent vision model as an adversarial auditor. It samples the clip cut-aware (evenly for global checks, plus three consecutive frames across each seam for temporal checks), prepends the character reference so it can compare the recast's build to the canonical one, and grades against a checklist — anatomy, smearing, ghosting, proportion drift vs the reference, color-space match, lighting match, framing. Its prompt tells it to assume there are defects and find them.

# exits non-zero if the verdict says FAIL — CI-friendly, blocks the deliverable
audit.py CLIP.mp4 --type recast --ref character.png --chunks <seam frames>

On the rejected recast, it independently caught everything we'd found by eye — the stretched proportions, the bad-blend ghosting at a seam, the color-space mismatch — plus things nobody caught: a melted eye, a warped shoe, and the baked-in watermark, read straight off the pixels. An independent model out-caught all of us.

The honest caveat: the auditor tiles three consecutive frames of a moving subject side-by-side, and sometimes reads that legitimate motion as "ghosting." The fix is to label each tile with its timestamp so the model knows they're distinct moments. A QA tool that cries wolf on real motion trains you to ignore it.

Part 3

Putting the character in a world: two tools, one job.

Recasting the character in the real scene is the easy, consistent case. The moment you want a themed world, you hit a fork. We bake-off'd two very different tools — same input, same instruction ("put this character in a new world, match the lighting, don't change the character"):

Dedicated relight toolGenerative video-edit
Treats the subject asPixels to preserveA thing to re-imagine
Identity / proportionsHeld pose-for-poseDrifted — warped face, extra finger
MotionPreserved exactlyRe-choreographed
Verdict for this jobRight toolWrong tool

The generative model produced a beautiful, different character. For subject-preserving work, that's a failure — it re-dreamed the very thing we spent all of Part 1 locking down. The lesson isn't "one tool is better." It's that they do different jobs: one is a compositor, the other a motion generator. That reframes the workflow — the expensive step (nailing identity + performance) happens once; then a surgical relight tool becomes a cheap, repeatable "swap the world" knob on top. Recast once, re-environment many.

Except the re-environment step drifts. Which is the real subject of this post.

Part 4 · the hard problem

Generative video has no volume.

When we put the character into a themed world in chunks, the world rearranged itself at every chunk boundary. The character was seamless across the joins; the world was not. The reason is fundamental:

Every frame is sampled from a distribution, not rendered from a world. Space drifts because there is no space — only correlated 2D guesses.

In 3D you get spatial consistency for free: a volume — geometry, camera, lights — persists, and every frame is a render of the same thing. Generative models have no volume. That single absence is why AI environments wander and the camera can't be trusted.

Why the real-scene recast doesn't drift: reality was the volume. A real camera moved through a real 3D space, and the recast preserved that background — a persistent volume, for free. The themed world drifted precisely because we threw the volume away and asked the model to invent a new one every few seconds.

The fix: bolt a volume back on as conditioning. There's a spectrum of how much you inject:

LevelWhat you injectConsistency
1 · Temporal contextPrior frames as init for the next chunkReduces drift; no real geometry
2 · 2D controlPer-frame depth / edge / pose mapsPer-frame hint; weak cross-frame 3D
3 · 3D scaffold from footageA point cloud / encoded source, re-rendered along the camera pathPersistent geometry + camera
4 · Full 3D proxyAn authored / captured scene → depth passes → restyleMaximum — a real volume

Level 3 is the clever middle, and there's a real technique for it. The key control node takes a render_latent and an optional render_mask, and feeds a 3D-consistent embedding into the sampler:

control_embed
    render_latent  ← encode( THE ORIGINAL FOOTAGE )   # the perspective anchor
    render_mask    ← (inverse subject matte)          # regenerate background only
    strength / start% / end%                          # how hard the scaffold binds
    → sampler

Two facts do all the work. One: the render_latent is just the encoded source footage — the model uses the real clip's structure to lock camera, perspective, and parallax. The volume is derived from reality for free. Two: the render_mask is the scope knob — wire it to the inverse of a subject matte and you regenerate the background while leaving the subject untouched. Because every frame is anchored to one source-derived scaffold, the per-chunk drift collapses.

The honest limit: you restyle the real volume; you don't invent a new one. The scaffold is the real scene's depth layout, so the new world inherits the footage's geometry. Wonderful for consistency, respects real parallax — but geometry the footage lacks still has to be authored. Conditioning can hold a volume; it can't invent one.

The meta-lesson

The moat is the volume you put back.

Every problem here is a symptom of the same thing: generative models are brilliant at look and blind to space and time. So the engineering isn't in the model — it's in the scaffolding around it: verification (an independent auditor, because the model won't tell you it drifted), preservation (surgical tools for subject-preserving work, not generative ones), and a reintroduced volume (spatial consistency comes from geometry you inject).

3D gives you a persistent world and total control, at the cost of building everything by hand. Generative AI gives you the look for free, and takes the world away. The tools worth building are the ones that hand the world back — a stable, perspective-correct scene a professional can trust, with the model doing only the paint. That's the moat. Not the model — the volume you put back underneath it.
