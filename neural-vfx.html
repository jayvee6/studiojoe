Neural VFX · Case study

Restyle is a filter.
Recast is a performance.

A dancer recast as Chun-Li. A phone-clip dance moved wholesale into the Mushroom Kingdom — not a style pass, a performance transfer. This is the whole pipeline: every stage, the tool behind it, the dead ends, and the one law that turned out to govern all of it.

Fire Mario in the Mushroom Kingdom — full-scene SCAIL-2 restyle

The thesis

Two operations that feel the same and aren't.

A restyle and a recast look like the same job wearing different clothes. They are not. One is a per-pixel function; the other has to synthesize a subject that doesn't exist and hold it together across frames, poses, and cuts. The anime style pass worked on the first try. Holding one specific character — her hands, her eyes, her world — fought back the whole way, and that gap is the entire story.

Restyle · a filter

Repaint the whole world

A per-pixel map: every pixel gets a styled version of itself. Identity-free, local, and temporally stable for free — the input video is already coherent, so dancers can cross, overlap, anything. A full-frame restyle model repaints the entire frame.

wan2.1_14B_SCAIL_2 — full-frame restyle

Recast · a performance

Keep the silhouette, swap the subject

A subject recast synthesizes a new character and composites it over the real scene. The character's pixels don't exist — they're hallucinated fresh every frame. It keeps the driver's silhouette, and that silhouette leaks: stray hair, a buff torso, identity drift.

Wan 2.2 Animate 14B — subject recast / composite

The method-selection rule: restyle repaints the whole world; recast keeps the driver's silhouette. Which one you reach for decides everything downstream — including whether you keep the real scene or get to build a new one.

The journey

Retrospectives live on the dead ends.

The credibility of a pipeline is the list of things that broke on the way to it. Here is the honest arc — what we tried, what died, and the pivots that followed.

Died

Per-frame Nano-Banana

A phenomenal single-keyframe engine with no temporal model — linework and watercolor boil frame to frame. It also obeys prompt wardrobe literally: a poisoned prompt invented a whole character. Rule born: read the source frame before prompting.

Died

EBSynth optical-flow propagation

Great for slow motion — talking heads, pans. It melts on fast dance footage: huge inter-keyframe motion breaks the warp and the blend becomes a triple exposure. Dead for anything with real choreography.

Won

AnimateDiff V2V, as a restyle

Coherent by construction — every frame is denoised from the real source latent. This was the filter that worked, and it seeded the whole restyle-vs-recast insight.

Won

SCAIL-2, driven by a reference

The breakthrough: a full-frame restyle where one stylized character sheet plus two prompts recasts the whole clip. The Chun-Li pass came out with her hands, her proportions, her identity — first real try. The only things that change per style are the reference image and the prompts.

Died

Prompting against the reference

On the distilled low-CFG path the prompt is a whisper. We asked for the Mushroom Kingdom in text while the reference sat on a grey studio backdrop — and the render stayed grey, run after run. The reference's own background out-votes any world you describe.

Fixed

The face-forward reference

Characters whose identity is a costume recast first-try. Characters whose identity is a face drift toward a generic prior — until the reference shows that face large, sharp, and front-on. Crop the face, upscale it, hand the model something to actually copy. Identity locks.

The law

The reference drives everything

Face, hair, material, lighting, the background, the world — every failure traced to the reference, and so did every fix. Build the world into the reference and it holds in every frame at every length. That law reorganized the whole pipeline around one question: what's in the ref?

The experiment · World control

One dance, five worlds. The reference won.

Same character, same dance, five strategies for getting the Mushroom Kingdom into the frame — a controlled shootout, about 93 credits of evidence. Two strategies gambled, one half-worked, and the two that carried the world inside the reference held it in every frame. The moment you demand control instead of "generate something cool," you land back on classical VFX discipline: build the shot, don't beg for it.

V1

Prompt + length

  • The historical recipe — world prompt at 81 frames
  • The world forms, but dissolves in — grey for 1.4s of a 3s clip
  • Character excellent; the world reads as wallpaper
Verdict — works, unreliably. Not a lever.

V2

Zoom-out alone

  • Pad the driver — subject at ~50% height, wide-shot composition
  • The probe rendered a gorgeous full vista
  • The full render collapsed back to grey
Verdict — a slot machine. Composition ≠ world.

V3

The better prompt

  • Open-vista world description, targeted negatives
  • Never formed — at any point, in any frame
  • The reference's grey backdrop won every run
Verdict — the prompt is a whisper.

V4

World in the reference

  • The character composited into a frame of the target world
  • The world held in all 89 frames, at every length
  • But a different world grammar than the series' look
Verdict — deterministic. The mechanism, proven.

V5

The continuity reference

  • Reference = a frame of the previous good shot
  • Same caps, same light, same palette — held every frame
  • Cuts with the established series by construction
Verdict — the flagship. Continuity beats beauty.

The next question · Spatial control

Win the world, then hold it still.

The reference decides which world. It doesn't decide where the world is. Regenerate a themed environment in chunks and it quietly rearranges itself between them — a mushroom here, then three feet left — and the camera can't be trusted. The reason is the whole difference between this and 3D: generative video has no persistent volume. Every frame is sampled, not rendered from a scene, so space drifts because there is no space. So the next experiment, about 7 credits of it: can you anchor the world's geometry to the real footage instead of re-inventing it?

You can't get consistency from the prior. You bolt a volume back on as a control signal — and the honest question is whether a cheap one works.

H

The hypothesis

  • Feed the encoded source clip as a 3D-consistency anchor (Uni3C)
  • So the new world inherits the real camera and parallax
  • Mask the background so only the world regenerates, subject held
On paper — reintroduce the volume for free.

✓

What held

  • The Mushroom Kingdom came out temporally stable
  • No per-frame boil, no chunk-to-chunk rearrangement
  • A shared latent anchor genuinely helps continuity
Verdict — a real signal. Continuity, yes.

✗

What didn't

  • On a source dolly, the output camera stayed locked
  • The subject-preserving mask? Unimplemented in the node itself
  • Encoded RGB is a picture, not a scaffold — no parallax transported
Verdict — the shortcut fails. Honestly.

The lesson rhymes with the rest of this post. The reference carried the world's look; its space needs real geometry, not a re-encoded frame. Done right, the anchor is a point-cloud guidance video rendered along the shot's actual camera path — and the subject is held by a separate matte composite, the classical way. Conditioning can hold a volume. It still can't invent one. Which is exactly where neural VFX keeps landing: back on the discipline of building the shot.

The resolution · Build the bones

So we built one. The camera moved.

We grey-boxed a scene in Blender — blocks at three depths, a deliberate camera truck — rendered it along that move, and fed that to the control instead of the footage. This time the generated frames tracked the move: foreground slid across, background held, parallax reproduced. Where the re-encoded footage stayed locked (first→last similarity 0.84), the authored volume transported the camera (0.76, matching the render), and a single strength dial turned the effect on and off. About five credits of proof. The volume was the missing lever.

You don't have to model a world. You model its bones — the massing and the camera. The neural model paints the rest.

One honest catch, and its fix. At the strength that moves the camera, the grey-box dominates — the first pass came out as moving blocks, not the temple we prompted. So we split the two jobs: a themed anchor frame carries the look, the volume carries only the motion. It worked — a stone-temple world that trucks left with depth-correct parallax, the foreground sliding past the background towers, the look seeded entirely by the anchor. Themed and moving. It isn't perfectly clean yet — the carved detail shimmers during the move, the polish a tighter anchor and more sampler steps would buy. But the shape of the answer is proven, and it's the same shape as everything else here: authored geometry for space, the neural model for surface. Classical VFX bones. Neural skin.

The classic VFX breakdown reel, neural edition — the same seven seconds of one dance, at every stage of the pipeline. The plate is decomposed into the departments a studio would recognize: a mocap pass, a roto pass, a layout pass — each one a neural model doing a craftsperson's job — and then the performance transfer that puts Chun-Li inside all of it. Nothing in the final frame is untracked: the motion came off the plate, the silhouette off the matte, the space off the depth. That's the difference between generating a video and making a shot — every pass is a decision you can inspect, swap, or grade.

The full toolchain

Eight tools. What each does, why it's here, the gotcha.

Every tool maps onto a classical VFX department. The gotchas below are the ones that actually cost time — the practical meat you only learn by hitting the wall.

SCAIL-2 wan2.1_14B_SCAIL_2_fp16 · WanSCAILToVideo

Full-frame restyle driven by one reference image plus the driver's performance — animation, lighting, and world in a single pass.

Why it's here
The engine behind every recast on this page: it repaints the character and the world while keeping the driver's motion — the performance transfer itself.
The gotcha
It runs a distilled low-CFG path, so the prompt is a whisper — the reference drives everything, including its own background. pose_strength 1.0→0.8 fixes foreshortened giant hands; expect settle frames at segment cold-starts.

SAM3 video segmentation + tracking

Point- and text-prompted segmentation that propagates across frames — the roto and traveling-matte department.

Why it's here
Mattes the rendered character off its backdrop for background-swap composites, and isolates subjects cleanly before any pose or identity pass.
The gotcha
Matte the render, not the driver — a recast character rarely shares the driver's proportions, so a driver-shaped matte clips it. Mask jobs OOM past ~30 frames; downscale first.

Flux Kontext Pro reference generation + editing

High-quality image generation that also accepts an input image — the model-sheet artist for a reference-driven pipeline.

Why it's here
Builds and edits the references that drive SCAIL — including compositing a character into a target-world frame. Permissive where Gemini hard-blocks franchise characters.
The gotcha
It occasionally signs its work — a stray signature squiggle in a corner. Crop it. Framing a request as a "rigging reference / model sheet" clears moderation that a character name won't.

Real-ESRGAN x4plus per-frame spatial upscale

Purely spatial 4× upscaling, frame by frame — the optical-printer blowup.

Why it's here
Turns a tiny face crop from source art into a usable face-forward reference — the cheap fix that locks a drifting identity.
The gotcha
"Purely spatial" is a feature: no temporal smoothing means deliberate wobble and animation texture survive the blowup untouched.

Nano-Banana 2 Gemini 3.1 Flash Image

Single-frame generation plus multi-image identity transfer — the keyframe and continuity artist.

Why it's here
Still the strongest single-keyframe engine for original characters and neutral-pose normalization passes.
The gotcha
It refuses copyrighted characters and real-person likenesses outright — which is exactly why Flux sits beside it in the kit. And it obeys prompt wardrobe literally; read the source frame before prompting.

AnimateDiff V2V in-place restyle

Denoises every frame from the real source latent — coherent by construction. The engine that stylizes your subject instead of swapping it.

Why it's here
The other half of engine-selection: SCAIL recasts a dancer into a character; AnimateDiff keeps the subject's identity and changes the material. Same request in English, two different jobs.
The gotcha
Denoise 0.5 + FreeInit 3 + wider context overlap stabilizes the subject, but the background still boils — no ControlNet on it. The real fix is compositing the subject over the original stable plate.

Blender headless reference renders

Renders character references straight from a 3D model — orthographic camera, controlled light, any framing. The turnaround artist.

Why it's here
When a rigged model of the character exists, a rendered reference beats any upscaled crop: accurate, high-res, artifact-free, and reusable for every future shot.
The gotcha
The reference carries its lighting into the render — a flat studio-lit ortho pass flattened a character's signature hair. Light the reference the way you want the shot lit.

FFmpeg · NumPy · PIL plate, comp, finish, audio

Plate prep, matte compositing, watermark and cold-start repair, audio mux — comp and DI.

Why it's here
The unglamorous spine — and the cheapest department. A watermark ghost in the last nine frames is a corner delogo, not a re-render; settle frames are a trim.
The gotcha
Trust decode counts, not container metadata — one driver's header claimed 167 frames; it had 89. Every downstream frame budget was wrong until ffprobe -count_frames said so.

What we learned · SCAIL

The reference is the API.

Everything the model honors rides in on the reference image — the face, the hair, the material, the lighting, the world. The prompt negotiates; the reference decides. Once that clicked, control stopped meaning "prompt harder" and started meaning what it has always meant in VFX: art-direct the inputs. Build the reference like a model sheet crossed with a location scout, and the shot follows.

Continuity, by construction

The last good shot is the next reference

A series has an established look, and every new shot is judged against it. So the strongest reference for shot N+1 is a frame of shot N — the character, palette, world design, and light arrive together, and the new render cuts with the old one without a single matched-grading pass. Dailies as an input, not just a review.

V5 — referenced from the previous shot's frame 140

One boolean

On a green screen, or in the world

SCAIL's replacement_mode = False conditions the entire frame — a full-scene restyle, e.g. Fire Mario in the Mushroom Kingdom. Flip it to True and it's subject-only, with the background passing through. The stock scail2_firemario_4seg.json workflow ships replacement_mode = True — subject-only, the green-screen default — and our runs flip it to False for the full-scene restyle. One setting is the whole difference between "on a green screen" and "in the world."

replacement_mode=False — the whole world converts

We were doing VFX the whole time

Every technique had a craft ancestor.

AI didn't replace the pipeline. It replaced the labor of each stage — the roto artist, the in-betweener, the matte painter, the mocap tech — and left the architecture standing. The architecture is the invariant.

What we builtCraft ancestor
SAM3 subject mattes→Rotoscoping (Fleischer, 1915) + traveling mattes
Face-forward character references→Model sheets / character turnarounds
The continuity reference from the last shot→The continuity supervisor + dailies
SCAIL driven by the dancer's performance→Performance pass driven by motion capture
World built into the reference→Matte painting + the location scout
The five-variant world shootout→Wedge tests / lineup before the take
Corner delogo, trims, audio mux→The compositing stack + DI finish

The one place AI genuinely diverges and the craft still wins: the 12 principles of animation — anticipation, follow-through, timing, weight, staging. Neural models reproduce driving motion but have no sense of intentional timing. The plumbing is absorbed; the performance is still human.

The principle · Authorship

The auteurs who reject AI still use After Effects.

Christopher Nolan and Hideo Kojima reject generative AI — but they embrace After Effects, Premiere, motion capture, photogrammetry, custom engines. Kojima is one of the most tech-forward creators alive; his rejection isn't anti-technology. It's a line drawn in exactly one place: the tool may execute the decision, never make it.

Instrument · embraced

The tool executes the decision

After Effects never decides what the shot looks like — the artist does, and the tool renders that decision faster, cleaner, at scale. Every frame is still authored. A brush, a camera, a compositor amplify intent; they don't originate it.

After Effects · Premiere · mocap · photogrammetry · engines

Author · rejected

The tool makes the decision

Generative AI, as it's pitched, decides everything from a prompt — a slot machine where the authorship used to be. That's the rejection, and it isn't about the technology. It's about who's holding the pen. Flawless and authorless still reads as slop.

prompt in, movie out — the ghostwriter model

Change the model — human authors, AI executes — and the rejection evaporates. Build the instrument, not the author, and you become the next After Effects, not the thing they reject. Everything on this page obeys that rule: the human keeps the keyframes, the timing, the performance.

The finished work · SCAIL-2

Three recasts. One restyle model.

The experiment above earned the method. These are the clips it produced — each a real SCAIL-2 render off real driving footage: full-scene restyles where the whole world converts, and a performance held through fast choreography. Tap any clip to watch it loop.

How it works

Five moves, plain-English.

Under the polish, every clip above rides the same five steps. No node graph required to follow the idea.

01

Full-frame restyle

SCAIL-2 denoises the whole frame from the real source, so the world stays coherent frame to frame. Flip replacement_mode to restyle the entire scene or just the subject.

02

Multi-subject crossing

Two dancers that cross paths get their own tracked colour via SCAIL2ColoredMask — each is repainted as a distinct object, so the crossing stops collapsing into an identity switch.

03

Boundary-aware pose

The pose driver is fenced to each subject's own matte, so a limb reaching across the frame is read against the right body instead of bleeding onto its neighbour.

04

Identity lock

InsightFace embeddings pin each character's face across the shot, so the same two leads resolve every frame instead of drifting into a fresh face on every cut.

05

Animation-smear finish

A light smear pass adds motion-blur streaks on fast limbs — the in-betweener's trick — so quick moves read with weight instead of strobing frame to frame.

The findings · Playbook

One law, paid for in credits: the reference drives everything.

On the distilled low-CFG path these models run at, the text prompt is a whisper — the reference image dictates the output. Face, hair, material, lighting, even the background. Learned the hard way: a full-body reference gave the costume but a stranger's face; a grey-studio Mario reference kept erasing the Mushroom Kingdom no matter what the prompt begged for. Every failure traced back to the same place. So does every fix.

Fight the ref · slot machine

Prompting against the reference

A five-variant shootout, same character, same dance: the world prompt never beat a grey-background reference. Longer renders let the world dissolve in — half the clip still grey. A wide-shot driver produced a gorgeous probe, then collapsed back to grey in the full render. Non-deterministic, every time.

prompt ✗ · length ~ · zoom-out alone 🎰

Fix the ref · deterministic

Building the world into the reference

Put the target world in the reference's own background and it held in every frame, at every length, both times it was tried. The same bleed that poisoned the grey renders, weaponized. The face fix is the same law: give the model a large, front-facing face to copy and identity locks.

world-in-ref ✓ · face-forward ref ✓ — every frame, every run

Continuity beats beauty. Once a series exists, the next shot is judged against the established look — so the best reference for shot N+1 is a frame of shot N. Self-referencing references make continuity a property of the pipeline, not a hope. That's the difference between filmmaking and pulling a slot machine lever.

RULE 01

Cast for the costume

Characters whose identity is an outfit (Mario, Chun-Li) recast first-try. Characters whose identity is a face drift generic — until the reference shows that face large and front-on. Best source: render it from a 3D model.

RULE 02

The driver is the ceiling

A tight, static phone clip can only grow wallpaper worlds. Wide shots give the world room; parallax is the driver's camera motion surviving the restyle. Cinematography in, cinematography out.

RULE 03

Engine = intent

Recast a dancer into a character: restyle with a character reference. Claymate your own subject in place: a different engine entirely. Same word — "make it clay" — two different jobs.

RULE 04

Probe, then look

A cheap 30-frame probe gates every render, and a human eyeballs the frames — exit codes lie, probes can too. Two failed attempts on one approach means stop and rethink, not roll again.

RULE 05

Fix locally, not by re-rolling

A watermark ghost in the last nine frames is an ffmpeg delogo, not a re-render. Settle frames are a trim. The cheapest fix is the one that doesn't touch the sampler.

PROOF

One pipeline, repeatable

The same style-routing tool recast a dancer as Chun-Li and moved a phone-clip dance into the established Mushroom Kingdom — same recipe, different reference. Pick a driver, pick a look, get the shot.
